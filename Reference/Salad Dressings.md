# 🥗 Salad Dressings

> Part of [[Cooking Reference]]

Almost every salad dressing is the same four things in different proportions: **fat**, **acid**, **salt** and an **emulsifier** to hold the first two together, plus whatever accents you want on top. Once you know the ratios you never need a dressing recipe again, because you can build one from whatever is in the fridge and correct it by taste.

The split is mostly two ways, but there are really **three families**, separated by where the body comes from and, more usefully, by how you fix them when they go wrong.

| Family | Base ratio | Where body comes from | Texture | Thin it with | Best on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Vinaigrette** (oil and acid) | **3 parts oil : 1 part acid** | An emulsifier binding oil into acid | Loose to lightly clingy | More acid or a splash of water | Tender greens, tomatoes, grains, beans, roasted veg, slaws |
| **Creamy** | **4 parts creamy base : 1 part acid**, thinned to pour | The base itself, already an emulsion or a thick dairy | Coating, spoonable to pourable | Buttermilk, milk, water, thin yogurt | Sturdy greens, wedge and chopped salads, crunchy raw veg, cold potato and pasta salads |
| **Paste or purée** | **1 part paste : 1 part acid : 1–2 parts water**, plus oil to taste | The solids in the paste (sesame, nuts, miso, avocado) | Thick, silky, clings hard | **Water**, never more oil | Bitter greens, cabbage, cold noodles, grain bowls, roasted vegetables |

Two more are worth knowing but are variations rather than families of their own. A **warm dressing** is a vinaigrette built in the pan on rendered bacon fat or sizzled oil, poured over spinach or escarole to wilt it on purpose. A **thin soy and citrus dressing** is structurally a vinaigrette, just one where soy sauce is doing the job of the salt and adding umami, so it carries far less oil (often 1:1) and tastes balanced anyway.

> [!warning] Ratios are a starting point, not a law
> Acids differ in strength: most wine vinegars run about 6 to 7% acidity, cider vinegar around 5%, rice vinegar only 4 to 4.5%, and citrus juice is milder still but tastes sharper. So 3:1 with sherry vinegar can be aggressive while 2:1 with rice vinegar tastes flat. Mix to the ratio, then taste and adjust.

## The five building blocks

1. **Fat** carries flavour, coats the leaves and tames the acid.
2. **Acid** provides the brightness that makes a salad taste like a salad.
3. **Emulsifier** keeps them combined instead of separating in the bowl.
4. **Salt** is not optional and is the most common thing missing from a homemade dressing.
5. **Accents** (sweet, allium, umami, heat, herbs, spice) are what give it an identity.

Blocks 1 through 4 make a working dressing. Block 5 makes it yours.

### 🫒 Fats and oils

See [[Cooking Oils]] for the fuller picture. For dressings, smoke point is irrelevant and flavour is everything.

| Oil                                        | Character                         | How to use it                                                                       |
| ------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Extra virgin olive oil**                 | Fruity, peppery, sometimes bitter | The default. Whisk by hand rather than blending, since blending can turn it bitter  |
| **Mild or light olive oil**                | Soft, olive-ish but quiet         | When good EVOO would fight the other flavours                                       |
| **Neutral (grapeseed, canola, sunflower)** | Nearly flavourless                | When you want the acid and accents in front, and as the carrier for strong nut oils |
| **Avocado oil**                            | Buttery, very mild                | A richer neutral, good in creamy blends                                             |
| **Walnut, hazelnut, pistachio oil**        | Distinctly nutty, delicate        | Use as **1 part in 4** with a neutral oil. Fragile, keep refrigerated               |
| **Toasted sesame oil**                     | Loud, roasted                     | An accent, not a base. **1 to 2 tsp** in a 1/4 cup dressing                         |
| **Pumpkin seed oil**                       | Dark, nutty, unmistakable         | Same treatment as walnut oil, a small proportion                                    |
| **Bacon fat or rendered duck fat**         | Savoury, meaty                    | Warm dressings only, since it sets solid when cold                                  |
| **Mayonnaise**                             | Rich, already emulsified          | Counts as both fat and emulsifier, which is why creamy dressings are so stable      |

| -------------------------------------- | --------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |

| **Sherry vinegar**                     | Sharp                 | Nutty, oxidized, complex                  | Frisée, roasted vegetables, lentils, Spanish flavours             |

| **Lemon juice**                        | Bright                | Floral, immediate, fades fast             | Greens, seafood, herbs, Mediterranean and Middle Eastern flavours |
| **Lime juice**                         | Bright                | Sharper and more perfumed than lemon      | Latin, Thai and Vietnamese flavours, avocado, corn, mango         |
| **Orange or grapefruit juice**         | Weak                  | Sweet, needs help                         | Combine with vinegar or lemon, or reduce it first to concentrate  |
| **Yuzu or sudachi**                    | Bright                | Intensely aromatic                        | Use in small amounts alongside rice vinegar                       |
| **Verjus**                             | Mild                  | Grapey, gentle, wine-friendly             | When vinegar would clash with the wine you are serving            |
| **Buttermilk, yogurt, kefir**          | Mild                  | Tangy and creamy at once                  | Doing double duty as the acid and the creamy base                 |
| **Pickle or caper brine, kraut juice** | Sharp and salty       | Already seasoned                          | Free acid with built-in salt, so hold back on salting             |

Citrus zest is worth adding whenever you use the juice. The aromatic oils in the peel survive in the fridge far better than the juice's brightness does.

### 🧷 Emulsifiers

An emulsifier coats the oil droplets so they cannot merge back together. Without one, oil and vinegar separate in about a minute. With one, a dressing holds for hours or weeks.

| Emulsifier                             | Amount per 1/4 cup (60 ml) dressing | Holding power  | Notes                                                                      |
| -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | -------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Dijon mustard**                      | 1/2 to 1 tsp                        | Strong         | The workhorse. Adds flavour and sharpness as well as stability             |
| **Whole grain mustard**                | 1 to 2 tsp                          | Medium         | Better texture, slightly weaker emulsion                                   |
| **Egg yolk**                           | 1 yolk per 3/4 cup oil              | Strongest      | Very rich and thick. Raw egg, so keep it cold and eat it within a few days |
| **Mayonnaise**                         | 1 to 2 tsp                          | Strong         | An easy way to make a vinaigrette creamy without dairy tang                |
| **Honey, maple syrup**                 | 1/2 to 1 tsp                        | Weak to medium | Emulsifies a little and sweetens a lot                                     |
| **Tahini or nut butter**               | 1 tbsp                              | Strong         | Also adds real body, so treat it as a paste dressing                       |
| **Miso**                               | 1 to 2 tsp                          | Strong         | Salt, umami and emulsification in one spoon. Ease off on added salt        |
| **Garlic mashed to a paste with salt** | 1 small clove                       | Medium         | The salt crystals break the garlic down and help it disperse               |
| **Yogurt**                             | 1 tbsp                              | Medium         | Contributes tang and thickness                                             |
| **Grated Parmesan or Pecorino**        | 1 tbsp                              | Medium         | Salty and savoury, also thickens                                           |
| **Jam, marmalade, tomato paste**       | 1 tsp                               | Medium         | Pectin and pulp both help, plus flavour                                    |
| **Aquafaba (chickpea liquid)**         | 1 tbsp                              | Medium         | Vegan, flavour-neutral                                                     |
| **Xanthan gum**                        | A tiny pinch, 1/8 tsp per cup       | Indefinite     | How bottled dressings never separate. Easy to overdo into sliminess        |

## Building your own

The method matters as much as the ratio, and it is the same three moves every time.

1. **Season the acid first.** Put the acid, the salt, the emulsifier and any minced allium in the bowl or jar and whisk. Salt dissolves in acid and not in oil, and giving shallot or garlic **10 minutes** to sit in the acid takes the raw bite off it.
2. **Add the fat.** Drizzle it in slowly while whisking for the most stable result, shake it in a sealed jar for speed, or use a blender for the tightest emulsion (but see the olive oil note below).
3. **Taste it on a leaf, not off a spoon.** A dressing tasted alone is always too sharp and too salty. Dip a piece of whatever you are dressing and judge it that way. Then adjust and taste again.

Pick a fat, an acid, an emulsifier and two or three accents from a single row and you will get something coherent. Mixing across rows works too, but check that the accents belong in the same kitchen before you commit a whole batch.

### Fixing a dressing

| Problem                      | Fix                                                                                                                  |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Too sharp or sour            | More oil, a little sweetener, or a spoon of creamy base                                                              |
| Flat and boring              | Salt first, then acid. Almost always salt                                                                            |
| Greasy or dull on the tongue | More acid and more salt, not more seasoning                                                                          |
| Too thick                    | **Water** (or buttermilk for creamy). Adding oil makes it heavier, not thinner                                       |
| Too thin                     | A little more emulsifier, or a spoonful of mayo, yogurt or tahini                                                    |
| Bitter                       | Often blended extra virgin olive oil. A pinch of sugar or honey helps, but next time whisk the EVOO in by hand       |
| Separated                    | Whisk in 1/2 tsp Dijon, or a few drops of water, and re-emulsify. A jar and 20 seconds of shaking fixes most of them |
| Harsh raw garlic or onion    | Let it sit in the acid for 10 minutes before adding oil, or switch to the powdered form                              |

### 🥄 Key tips
- **A 3:1 vinaigrette is the anchor.** 3 tbsp oil, 1 tbsp acid, 1/2 tsp Dijon and a good pinch of salt makes about 1/4 cup, which dresses a salad for four.
- **Use less dressing than you think.** The leaves should glisten with no puddle in the bottom of the bowl. Add half, toss, then judge.
- **Dress at the last possible moment**, and toss from the bottom up with your hands or two large spoons. Acid and salt start collapsing tender greens within minutes.
- **Sturdy greens want it earlier.** Kale, cabbage and radicchio actually improve with 10 to 20 minutes of sitting in a sharper 2:1 dressing.
- **Whisk extra virgin olive oil by hand.** High-speed blending shatters its polyphenols and can turn a good oil noticeably bitter. Blend the neutral oil, then whisk the EVOO in at the end.
- **Strong oils are accents.** Toasted sesame, walnut and pumpkin seed oils belong at roughly 1 part in 4, with a neutral oil doing the volume.
- **Watch the hidden salt.** Soy, miso, anchovy, Parmesan, capers and pickle brine all bring their own, so season last.
- **Make a big jar of the base** (oil, acid, mustard, salt) and flavour small amounts differently as you go. Herbs and fresh alliums are what shorten a dressing's life, so add them per meal.
- **A jar is the best tool.** Everything in, lid on, shake hard for 20 seconds. It emulsifies, stores and re-emulsifies in the same container.

### 🗄️ Storage
- **Vinaigrette, no fresh aromatics:** up to **2 to 3 weeks** in the fridge. With fresh garlic, shallot or herbs, **3 to 4 days**.
- **Olive oil solidifies when cold.** Let the jar stand on the counter 15 minutes, then shake it again.
- **Creamy dairy dressings:** **5 to 7 days**, or the earliest date on any of the bases, whichever comes first.
- **Raw egg yolk dressings:** **2 to 3 days**, and use pasteurized eggs if you are serving anyone who should avoid raw egg.
- **Tahini and nut butter dressings:** about **1 week**. They thicken considerably in the fridge, so loosen with water before serving.
- **Avocado based:** same day.
- **Never store garlic in oil at room temperature.** It is a genuine botulism risk. Keep any garlicky dressing refrigerated.

> [!tip] Rule of thumb
> **3 parts fat, 1 part acid, 1 emulsifier, salt.** Season the acid first, add the fat slowly, taste it on a leaf. Then fix it with the three levers: fat for sharpness, acid for dullness, water for thickness.
