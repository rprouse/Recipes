# 🍲 Stocks

> Part of [[Cooking Reference]]

The French call these *fonds de cuisine*, the foundations of cooking, and the name is the whole idea: a stock is not a dish, it is the base liquid that soups, sauces, braises and risottos are built on. Two of the five sauces in [[Mother Sauces]] are nothing more than a stock plus a roux, which is why the stock does most of the work in deciding whether the finished sauce tastes of anything.

There are four classical base stocks, or five if you count vegetable stock, which the old brigade largely ignored.

| Stock | French name | Bones treated how | Simmer time | Feeds into |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **White stock** | *fond blanc* | Raw, often blanched, never browned | Chicken 3–4 h, veal 6–8 h | Velouté, cream soups, risotto, poaching liquid |
| **Brown stock** | *fond brun*, *estouffade* | Roasted dark, with roasted mirepoix and tomato | Veal or beef 8–12 h | Espagnole, demi-glace, braises, pan sauces |
| **Fish stock** | *fumet* | Raw bones sweated in butter with white wine | 30–45 min only | Fish velouté, bisque, poaching liquid |
| **Court bouillon** | *court bouillon* | No bones at all: water, acid, aromatics | 20–30 min | Poaching fish, shellfish, offal, vegetables |
| **Vegetable stock** | *fond de légumes* | No bones, vegetables only | 45 min to 1 h | Anywhere a light, meatless stock is wanted |

## Stock is not broth

The words get used interchangeably on labels, but in a kitchen they are two different things.

| | Stock | Broth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Made from | **Bones**, plus collagen-rich trim | **Meat** |
| Seasoned | No | Yes |
| Body | Gelatin, so it sets to a jelly when cold | Thin, stays liquid |
| Flavour | Deep and neutral, a background | Distinctly meaty, a foreground |
| Purpose | An ingredient | Something you drink or eat as-is |

A good stock jiggles when cold. That gelatin is what gives a pan sauce its glossy, lip-coating quality, and it is the thing you cannot get from a carton. "Bone broth" sold for drinking is essentially a stock simmered a long time and then seasoned, so it sits between the two columns.

## The ratios

| Element | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| **Bones to water** | **1 : 2 by weight**, roughly 1 lb of bones per 1 quart (450 g per litre). The bones should stay just covered |
| **Mirepoix** | **10% of the bone weight**, in the classic **2 : 1 : 1** of onion, carrot, celery |
| **White mirepoix** | Onion, celery, leek and a little parsnip, no carrot, when you need the stock to stay pale |
| **Sachet, per 4 quarts (4 L)** | 1 bay leaf, 2 sprigs thyme, 6 parsley stems, 1/2 tsp peppercorns, 1 garlic clove |
| **Salt** | **None.** A stock gets reduced, sometimes to a tenth of its volume, so season the finished dish instead |
| **Yield** | Expect to lose roughly a quarter to a third of the water to evaporation over a long simmer |

| Stock | Simmer | Why that long |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fish (fumet)** | 30–45 min | Small bones give up everything fast, then turn bitter and gluey |
| **Vegetable** | 45 min to 1 h | Past an hour the vegetables go muddy and flat |
| **Chicken** | 3–4 h | Enough for the collagen in wings, backs and feet |
| **Poultry and game** | 4–5 h | Denser bones than chicken |
| **Veal** | 6–8 h | Knuckle and neck are the most collagen-rich bones there are |
| **Beef** | 8–12 h | Heavy, dense bones, and brown stock is usually beef or veal |
| **Remouillage** | Same as the parent | A second, weaker extraction from bones you already used |

## White stock (fond blanc)

The workhorse. Raw bones, cold water, aromatics and time, with nothing browned at any point, so it stays pale gold and tastes clean. Chicken is the everyday version; veal is the professional one, because veal knuckle produces so much gelatin that the stock sets like rubber and gives sauces real body. It is the liquid for velouté, for cream soups, for risotto, and for poaching almost anything.

**🛒 Ingredients**
- 4 lb (1.8 kg) chicken backs, wings and feet, or veal knuckle and neck bones cut into 3 inch pieces
- 3 1/2 quarts (3.5 L) cold water, plus more to top up
- 6 oz (170 g) onion, 3 oz (85 g) carrot, 3 oz (85 g) celery, all roughly chopped
- Sachet: 1 bay leaf, 2 sprigs thyme, 6 parsley stems, 1/2 tsp peppercorns, 1 garlic clove

**🥣 Steps**
1. Rinse the bones under cold water. For the clearest possible stock, **blanch** them: cover with cold water, bring to a boil, drain, and rinse the pot and the bones. You lose a little flavour and gain a lot of clarity.
2. Return the bones to the pot and cover with the cold water. Bring to a bare simmer slowly, over 30 minutes or so, and skim the grey foam off the surface as it collects.
3. Add the mirepoix and sachet. For chicken, add them in the last hour; for veal, after the first hour or two. Vegetables added at the start will be mush and taste of nothing by the end.
4. Simmer, uncovered, at 180 to 190°F (82 to 88°C): lazy bubbles breaking at the edge of the pot and nowhere else. **Never let it boil.** Top up with water if the bones start to poke out.
5. Strain through a fine sieve or a chinois lined with damp cheesecloth. Let it drip. **Do not press on the solids**, since that is what clouds a clear stock.
6. Cool it fast in an ice bath or in shallow containers, then refrigerate overnight and lift the solid fat cap off the top.

## Brown stock (fond brun)

Identical to white stock in structure, with one difference that changes everything: the bones and the mirepoix are roasted dark first. That is the entire distinction between the base of a velouté and the base of an espagnole. The roasting, the tomato and the long reduction give it colour, depth and a savoury edge that makes it the foundation of every classic red-meat sauce, usually by way of demi-glace.

**🛒 Ingredients**
- 5 lb (2.3 kg) veal or beef bones, cut into 3 inch pieces, including knuckle and marrow bones
- 8 oz (225 g) onion, 4 oz (115 g) carrot, 4 oz (115 g) celery, roughly chopped
- 3 tbsp tomato paste
- 1 cup (250 ml) dry red wine or water, for deglazing
- 4 quarts (4 L) cold water, plus more to top up
- Sachet: 1 bay leaf, 3 sprigs thyme, 8 parsley stems, 1/2 tsp peppercorns, 2 garlic cloves

**🥣 Steps**
1. Roast the bones in a single layer at 425°F (220°C) for 45 to 60 minutes, turning once, until they are deeply and evenly brown. Pale bones make pale, weak stock, so take them further than looks comfortable.
2. Add the mirepoix to the roasting pan for the last 20 minutes, then smear the tomato paste over everything and roast 10 minutes more, until the paste has darkened to rust.
3. Transfer the solids to the stockpot. Put the roasting pan on the stove, deglaze with the wine, and scrape up every bit of the browned fond. That scraping is a large part of the flavour, so do not skip it. Pour it all into the pot.
4. Add the cold water, bring slowly to a bare simmer, and skim.
5. Add the sachet and simmer 8 to 12 hours, skimming the fat and scum periodically and topping up the water as needed.
6. Strain through cheesecloth without pressing, cool quickly, and chill. Lift off the fat cap, which is excellent for roasting potatoes.

## Fish stock (fumet)

The fast one, and the one most easily ruined. Fish frames give up their flavour in half an hour, and past about 45 minutes they start contributing bitterness and a gluey texture instead. Use **lean white fish** only: sole, turbot, halibut, snapper, cod. Oily fish like salmon and mackerel make a stock that tastes rank. Rinse the bones properly, since blood and gills are what turn a fumet muddy and bitter.

**🛒 Ingredients**
- 2 lb (900 g) frames and heads from lean white fish, gills and any blood removed, rinsed well in cold water
- 2 tbsp butter
- 1 medium onion or 2 leeks, thinly sliced (no carrot, it tints the stock)
- 4 oz (115 g) mushroom trimmings (optional, they add depth)
- 1 cup (250 ml) dry white wine
- 1 1/2 quarts (1.5 L) cold water
- 6 parsley stems, 1 sprig thyme, 1 bay leaf, 1/2 tsp white peppercorns, 1 slice lemon

**🥣 Steps**
1. Melt the butter in a wide pot and sweat the onion or leek and the mushroom trimmings gently for 5 minutes, without letting them colour at all.
2. Add the fish bones, cover the pot, and sweat 5 more minutes, until the bones turn opaque and start releasing liquid.
3. Pour in the wine and let it bubble for 2 minutes.
4. Add the cold water to just cover, plus the herbs, peppercorns and lemon. Bring to a bare simmer and skim carefully.
5. Simmer **30 to 40 minutes at most**, uncovered, never boiling.
6. Strain gently through cheesecloth without pressing. Cool quickly and use within a couple of days, or freeze.

> [!tip] Shellfish variation
> Swap the fish bones for shrimp, crab or lobster shells and roast them first at 400°F (200°C) for 15 minutes. That gives you the base for a bisque. Shells can take a slightly longer simmer than fish bones, up to an hour.

## Court bouillon

The odd one out, because there are no bones in it at all. Court bouillon means "short broth", and it is exactly that: an aromatic, acidulated liquid simmered briefly and then used to poach something. It contributes flavour to the food rather than extracting flavour from it, so unlike the others it **is** salted. Traditional for poaching whole fish, shellfish, chicken breast, offal and vegetables like artichokes.

**🛒 Ingredients**
- 2 quarts (2 L) water
- 1 cup (250 ml) dry white wine, or 1/4 cup (60 ml) white wine vinegar
- 1 onion, sliced, 1 carrot, sliced, 2 celery ribs, sliced
- 1 lemon, sliced
- 2 bay leaves, 6 parsley stems, 1 sprig thyme
- 1 tbsp salt
- 1 tsp peppercorns

**🥣 Steps**
1. Combine everything except the peppercorns and bring to a simmer.
2. Simmer 20 to 30 minutes, adding the peppercorns in the last 10 minutes so they do not turn harsh.
3. Strain if you want a clean poaching liquid, or leave the aromatics in for a rustic presentation.
4. Poach at **160 to 180°F (71 to 82°C)**, which is well below a simmer. Boiling liquid tears delicate fish apart.
5. Cool whole poached fish or shellfish **in the liquid** so it stays moist and keeps absorbing flavour.

## Vegetable stock (fond de légumes)

Not a classical *fond*, but the most-used stock in a lot of modern kitchens. The rules are the reverse of the meat stocks: it wants a **short** simmer, because vegetables give up their flavour quickly and then start tasting stewed and muddy. It will never have body, since there is no collagen, so if you need a vegetarian stock with substance, lean on dried mushrooms, kombu or roasted vegetables for depth instead.

**🛒 Ingredients**
- 2 tbsp neutral oil or olive oil
- 2 onions, 2 leeks (including the greens), 2 carrots, 3 celery ribs, all roughly chopped
- 8 oz (225 g) mushrooms or mushroom trimmings
- 4 cloves garlic, smashed
- 1 tomato, chopped, or 1 tbsp tomato paste (optional)
- 3 quarts (3 L) cold water
- 8 parsley stems, 2 sprigs thyme, 1 bay leaf, 1/2 tsp peppercorns

**🥣 Steps**
1. Sweat all the vegetables in the oil over medium heat for 10 minutes, stirring, until they soften and smell sweet but have taken on no colour. For a darker, deeper stock, brown them properly instead and add the tomato paste.
2. Add the cold water and the herbs, and bring to a bare simmer.
3. Simmer **45 minutes to 1 hour only**, uncovered.
4. Strain without pressing on the vegetables, which would push starch and sediment through and cloud it.

> [!warning] Vegetables to keep out
> **Brassicas** (cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts) turn sulfurous. **Beets** dye everything. **Potato** and other starchy vegetables make it cloudy and can sour it. **Bell peppers** and **artichokes** dominate or turn bitter. Go easy on **carrot** too, since too much makes a stock cloyingly sweet.

## Above the base stocks

These are not separate stocks so much as what you do with one once you have it.

| | What it is | How it is used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Remouillage** | Literally "re-wetting": a second, weaker stock from bones you have already simmered once | Use it in place of water when you start the next batch, so nothing is wasted |
| **Demi-glace** | Equal parts espagnole and brown stock, reduced by half | The base of nearly every classic brown sauce. See [[Mother Sauces]] |
| **Glace de viande** | Brown stock reduced to roughly a tenth of its volume, dark and syrupy, solid when cold | A teaspoon transforms a pan sauce. Freeze in cubes |
| **Consommé** | Stock clarified with an egg-white raft (*clearmeat*: lean ground meat, egg whites, mirepoix, tomato) | Crystal-clear soup. Once the raft forms, never stir it |
| **Fortified stock** | Stock simmered again with fresh bones and mirepoix | When the first pass came out weaker than you wanted |

### 🔑 Key tips
- **Always start with cold water.** Hot water seizes the proteins at the surface of the bones and they never leave. Cold water draws them out gradually, and the impurities coagulate and rise where you can skim them off.
- **Never boil a stock.** Hold it at 180 to 190°F (82 to 88°C). Boiling emulsifies the fat into the liquid and churns particles into suspension, which is exactly how you get cloudy, greasy stock. This is the single most important rule on this page.
- **Skim, do not stir.** Stirring pushes the scum back down into the liquid. Take it off the top with a ladle.
- **Add the mirepoix late.** In the last hour of a long stock. Eight hours reduces vegetables to sludge and their flavour to nothing.
- **Never salt a stock.** A litre reduced to a cup concentrates the salt eightfold, and there is no way back.
- **Collagen is the goal, not meat.** Knuckles, feet, wings, necks, backs and skin are what set a stock. Veal knuckle is the gold standard; chicken feet are the cheap shortcut to the same effect.
- **Do not press the solids when straining.** Let gravity do it, even if you leave some liquid behind.
- **Cool it fast, and never on the stovetop overnight.** An ice bath, an ice paddle, or splitting it into shallow containers. A large hot pot put straight in the fridge sits in the danger zone for hours and warms everything around it.
- **Save your scraps.** A freezer bag of onion skins, carrot peel, celery tops, leek greens, parsley stems, mushroom stems and roast chicken carcasses is a free stock waiting to happen.
- **Reduce for storage.** If freezer space is tight, reduce the stock by three quarters, freeze it in ice-cube trays, and add water back later.

### 🗄️ Storage
- **Refrigerator:** 3 to 4 days. Leaving the solid fat cap on top seals the surface and buys you a day or two more, so skim it off only when you are about to use the stock.
- **Extending it:** bring it back to a rolling boil for 5 minutes every 3 days and it will keep going, though the flavour dulls each time. Freezing is better.
- **Freezer:** 6 months for meat stocks, 2 to 3 months for fumet, which fades faster. Label the type, because white chicken and white veal look identical frozen.
- **Ice-cube trays** for pan-sauce quantities, plus 2 cup containers for soups and braises.
- **Glace de viande** keeps for months in the fridge; it is concentrated enough to be self-preserving. Cut off cubes as needed.
- **Court bouillon** can be strained, refrigerated and reused two or three times for poaching, and it gets better each time.

> [!tip] Rule of thumb
> **1 part bones to 2 parts cold water, 10% mirepoix, no salt, and never a boil.** Brown or don't brown the bones, and simmer for as long as the bones are big: 40 minutes for fish, 4 hours for chicken, 8 or more for veal.
