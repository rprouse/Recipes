# 🥄 The Five French Mother Sauces

> Part of [[Cooking Reference]]

The mother sauces are the five base sauces of classical French cooking, codified by Auguste Escoffier in the early 1900s, building on Marie-Antoine Carême's earlier list of four. None of them is really meant to be eaten on its own. Each one is a **platform**: a liquid, a thickener and a long enough simmer to make something smooth and glossy, which you then turn into one of dozens of "small sauces" (*sauces filles*, the daughter sauces) by adding wine, aromatics, cheese, cream or a reduction. Learn five sauces, get fifty.

Escoffier's five are **Béchamel** (milk), **Velouté** (white stock), **Espagnole** (brown stock), **Hollandaise** (butter and egg yolk) and **Tomate** (tomato). Carême's original list had **Allemande** in place of Hollandaise; Escoffier demoted it to a child of velouté, which is where it still sits.

Three of the five are built on a stock, and that stock decides most of what the finished sauce tastes like, so it is worth making your own: see [[Stocks]] for the base stocks and their ratios.

| Sauce | Liquid | Thickener | Classic uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Béchamel** | Milk | White roux | Gratins, lasagna, croque monsieur, cheese sauces, creamed vegetables |
| **Velouté** | White stock (chicken, veal, fish) | Blond roux | Poached poultry and fish, vol-au-vents, pot pies, chicken fricassée |
| **Espagnole** | Brown stock (veal or beef) | Brown roux | Red meats, roasts, game, braises (usually via demi-glace) |
| **Hollandaise** | Melted butter | Egg yolk emulsion | Eggs Benedict, asparagus, poached fish, steak (as béarnaise) |
| **Tomate** | Tomatoes plus stock | Reduction (classically a roux too) | Pasta, gratins, poultry, eggs, braised meats |

## About roux

Three of the five are thickened with **roux**: equal parts by weight of fat and flour, cooked together so the flour loses its raw taste and its starch can swell in liquid. Weight is the accurate way to measure it, but in practice **equal tablespoons of butter and flour** gets you close enough.

How long you cook the roux is the whole game. The longer it goes, the more toasted and nutty the flavour becomes, and the **less** it thickens, because browning breaks down the starch. A brown roux has roughly half the thickening power of a white one, so brown sauces lean on long reduction as much as on flour.

| Roux | Cook time | Colour and smell | Used in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **White** | 2–3 min | No colour, smells faintly of raw pastry | Béchamel, cream soups |
| **Blond** | 5–8 min | Pale straw, smells of biscuit or popcorn | Velouté, fish veloutés, light gumbo |
| **Brown** | 20–45 min | Copper to milk chocolate, deeply nutty | Espagnole, Cajun gumbo and étouffée |

The mechanics, whichever colour you are after: melt the fat, whisk in the flour, and cook over medium-low heat, stirring constantly. Then add the liquid **warm** and in stages, whisking smooth before each addition. A hot roux plus cold liquid (or the reverse) is the usual cause of lumps. Once the sauce comes together it needs at least 10 minutes at a bare simmer to cook out the last of the floury taste.

> [!tip] Roux ratios per 2 cups (500 ml) of liquid
> **Thin** (soup base): 1 tbsp each butter and flour. **Medium** (most sauces, coats a spoon): 2 to 3 tbsp each. **Thick** (soufflé base, croquettes, binding a filling): 4 tbsp each.

## Béchamel

The white sauce: milk thickened with a white roux. It is the mildest of the five and the most useful in a home kitchen, because it becomes cheese sauce, it binds a lasagna, and it is the base of a gratin or a croque monsieur. A proper béchamel tastes of milk and butter with a faint background of onion and nutmeg, not of flour, so give it the full simmer.

**🛒 Ingredients**
- 2 cups (500 ml) whole milk
- 1/2 small onion, peeled, studded with 1 whole clove and 1 bay leaf (an *onion piqué*, optional but worth it)
- 3 tbsp (45 g) butter
- 3 tbsp (25 g) all-purpose flour
- Salt and white pepper
- A few scrapes of fresh nutmeg

**🥣 Steps**
1. Warm the milk with the onion piqué until steaming, then take it off the heat and leave it to infuse for 10 to 15 minutes.
2. Melt the butter in a heavy saucepan over medium-low heat. Whisk in the flour and cook for 2 to 3 minutes, stirring constantly, until it smells like warm pastry but has taken on no colour at all.
3. Discard the onion and add the warm milk a ladle at a time, whisking each addition completely smooth before adding the next.
4. Bring to a bare simmer and cook 10 to 15 minutes, stirring often and scraping the corners of the pan, until glossy and thick enough to coat the back of a spoon.
5. Season with salt, white pepper and nutmeg. For a flawless texture, pass it through a fine sieve. Press buttered parchment or plastic wrap onto the surface if it has to wait, or it will skin over.

### Mornay
Béchamel finished off the heat with grated Gruyère and Parmesan, roughly 1/2 cup total per 2 cups of sauce, often enriched with an egg yolk. The cheese sauce of macaroni, croque monsieur and vegetable gratins. Add the cheese off the heat so it melts rather than splits.

### Crème
Béchamel loosened with 1/2 cup of heavy cream and brightened with a squeeze of lemon. A simple, rich pour for poached fish, chicken or vegetables.

### Soubise
Béchamel with a purée of onions slowly sweated in butter until sweet and completely soft, about a pound of onions per 2 cups of sauce, then blended and sieved. Served with veal, lamb, eggs and roasted vegetables.

### Nantua
Béchamel enriched with cream and crayfish butter, with a garnish of crayfish tails. The classic sauce for quenelles de brochet (pike dumplings). Lobster or shrimp butter stands in easily.

### Moutarde
Béchamel whisked with 1 to 2 tbsp of Dijon mustard off the heat, since boiling mustard turns it bitter. Very good with pork chops, sausages, ham and boiled potatoes.

### Cheddar
The North American cousin of Mornay: sharp cheddar melted in, often with a pinch of dry mustard and cayenne. Macaroni and cheese, broccoli, nachos.

## Velouté

Velouté means "velvety", and that is exactly the point: a pale, silky sauce made by whisking warm [[Stocks|white stock]] into a blond roux. It is béchamel's savoury twin, and it takes its character entirely from the stock, so a chicken velouté, a veal velouté and a fish velouté (the last usually built on fumet) all behave the same but taste completely different. Almost nobody serves it plain. It exists to become suprême, allemande or normande.

**🛒 Ingredients**
- 2 cups (500 ml) white stock (chicken, veal or fish), warm
- 3 tbsp (45 g) butter
- 3 tbsp (25 g) all-purpose flour
- Salt and white pepper
- A squeeze of lemon juice

**🥣 Steps**
1. Melt the butter over medium-low heat, whisk in the flour, and cook 5 to 8 minutes to a blond roux: the colour of pale straw, smelling nutty and toasty. Stir the whole time so it colours evenly.
2. Add the warm stock a ladle at a time, whisking smooth after each addition.
3. Simmer gently for 20 to 30 minutes, skimming any foam or fat that rises and stirring so it does not catch. It should reduce slightly and turn glossy.
4. Strain through a fine sieve, then season with salt, white pepper and a little lemon. Keep it covered and warm; a velouté skins over as readily as a béchamel.

### Suprême
Chicken velouté simmered with mushroom trimmings, then reduced with heavy cream and finished with butter and lemon. Poured over poached or roasted chicken and used in vol-au-vents.

### Allemande (Parisienne)
Velouté thickened further with a *liaison* of egg yolk and cream, tempered in off the heat, then sharpened with lemon. Carême counted this as a mother sauce in its own right. Never let it boil once the yolks go in.

### Aurore
Velouté tinted and sweetened with tomato purée, about 2 tbsp per cup, and finished with butter, giving it a pale sunrise pink. Served with eggs, poultry and veal.

### Poulette
Allemande with sautéed mushrooms, chopped parsley and a good hit of lemon. Traditional with mussels, veal and, in Normandy, with tripe.

### Bercy
Fish velouté built on a reduction of minced shallots and dry white wine, finished with butter and parsley. The go-to sauce for poached or grilled white fish.

### Normande
Fish velouté enriched with cream, an egg yolk liaison and mushroom cooking liquor, sometimes with a splash of cider or Calvados. Sole normande is the dish it was invented for.

### Hongroise
Velouté with onions sweated in butter with sweet paprika, deglazed with white wine and finished with cream. Paprika needs gentle heat, since it scorches and turns bitter fast.

## Espagnole

The brown sauce, and the most laborious of the five: a dark brown roux, caramelized mirepoix, tomato and [[Stocks|brown veal stock]], simmered for hours and strained. On its own it is deliberately a bit rough and assertive. Its real job is to become **demi-glace**, which is equal parts espagnole and brown stock reduced by half, and demi-glace in turn is the base of nearly every classic red-meat sauce. Make a batch, reduce it, and freeze it in ice-cube trays.

**🛒 Ingredients**
- 6 cups (1.5 L) brown veal or beef stock, warm
- 6 tbsp (85 g) butter, beef fat or rendered pork fat
- 1/2 cup (65 g) all-purpose flour
- 2 cups mirepoix: 1 cup diced onion, 1/2 cup diced carrot, 1/2 cup diced celery
- 2 tbsp tomato paste, or 1/2 cup crushed tomato
- 1/2 cup (125 ml) dry red wine (optional)
- Sachet: 1 bay leaf, 2 sprigs thyme, 6 parsley stems, 8 peppercorns

**🥣 Steps**
1. Make a brown roux: melt the fat in a heavy pot, whisk in the flour, and cook over medium-low heat, stirring almost constantly, for 20 to 40 minutes until it is the colour of milk chocolate and smells like toasted nuts. Do not rush it and do not walk away, because it goes from brown to burnt quickly. Set aside.
2. In a second pan, brown the mirepoix in a little fat until the onions are deeply golden and the carrots have caught some colour.
3. Add the tomato paste and cook it, stirring, until it darkens to a rust colour, about 3 minutes. Deglaze with the red wine and reduce it almost dry.
4. Whisk the warm stock into the roux a ladle at a time until smooth, then add the mirepoix mixture and the sachet.
5. Simmer very gently, uncovered, for 2 hours, skimming the fat and scum off the surface regularly. It should reduce by about a third and coat a spoon lightly.
6. Strain through a fine sieve or chinois, pressing gently on the solids. Season at the very end, once the reduction has concentrated.
7. **For demi-glace:** combine the finished espagnole with an equal volume of brown stock and reduce by half, skimming as you go, until it is dark, syrupy and glossy.

### Demi-glace
Not a child sauce so much as the finished form of espagnole: equal parts espagnole and brown stock reduced by half. Deep, syrupy and intensely savoury, and the starting point for everything below.

### Bordelaise
Demi-glace with a reduction of dry red wine, minced shallots, thyme and cracked pepper, traditionally garnished with poached bone marrow and finished with butter. The steak sauce.

### Chasseur (Hunter's sauce)
Demi-glace with mushrooms and shallots sautéed in butter, white wine, chopped tomato and a finish of tarragon and parsley. Serve with chicken, veal or game.

### Robert
Demi-glace with onions sweated in butter, deglazed with white wine and vinegar, finished with Dijon mustard off the heat. The traditional partner for grilled pork.

### Charcutière
Sauce Robert with julienned cornichons stirred in at the last moment. Pork chops, again, and sausages.

### Madère (Madeira)
Demi-glace reduced with Madeira and finished with butter. Swap the Madeira for Port and it becomes sauce au porto, for sherry, sauce Xérès. Excellent with beef, ham and veal.

### Périgueux
Sauce Madère with chopped truffles and their soaking liquor. The classic sauce for beef Wellington and filet mignon.

### Piquante
Demi-glace with a sharp reduction of white wine and vinegar with shallots, finished with cornichons, capers, parsley and tarragon. Cuts through rich or fatty meats.

### Lyonnaise
Demi-glace with plenty of onions cooked slowly to golden, deglazed with white wine and vinegar. Steak, liver, leftover roast meats.

## Hollandaise

The odd one out: no roux, no stock, no simmering. Hollandaise is a warm emulsion of egg yolk and butter, held together by lecithin in the yolk and a little acid, the same way a mayonnaise is. That makes it the fastest of the five to make and the easiest to ruin. Two rules cover most failures: keep the heat gentle (yolks scramble around 160°F / 71°C) and add the butter slowly enough that each addition disappears before the next one goes in. It cannot be refrigerated and reheated, so make it just before serving.

**🛒 Ingredients**
- 3 large egg yolks
- 1 tbsp cold water
- 1 tbsp lemon juice, or a reduction of 2 tbsp white wine vinegar with a minced shallot
- 1 cup (225 g) butter, melted and warm, clarified for the most stable sauce
- Salt and a pinch of cayenne

**🥣 Steps**
1. Set a heatproof bowl over a pan of barely simmering water; the water must not touch the bowl. Whisk the yolks with the water and lemon juice.
2. Whisk constantly for 3 to 5 minutes until the mixture thickens, pales, roughly doubles in volume and leaves a ribbon trail when the whisk lifts. If it starts to feel hot to the touch, lift the bowl off for a few seconds and keep whisking.
3. Take the bowl off the heat. Add the warm butter drop by drop at first, whisking hard, then in a thin steady stream once the emulsion is clearly holding. If you clarified the butter, stop before the milky solids at the bottom.
4. Season with salt, cayenne and more lemon. Thin with a teaspoon of warm water if it is thicker than you want.
5. Hold it in a warm spot, around 120 to 140°F (50 to 60°C), for up to an hour. Hotter and it scrambles, colder and it sets.

> [!warning] If it breaks
> A split hollandaise is usually rescuable. Whisk in a tablespoon of very hot water, a drop at a time, and it will often come back. If it does not, start a fresh yolk with a teaspoon of water in a clean bowl and whisk the broken sauce into it slowly, the same way you added the butter.

### Béarnaise
Hollandaise built on a reduction of white wine vinegar, white wine, minced shallot, cracked pepper and tarragon stems instead of plain lemon, then finished with chopped fresh tarragon and chervil. The classic sauce for steak.

### Choron
Béarnaise with tomato purée or concentrated tomato paste whisked in, turning it pale coral. Steak, eggs and grilled fish.

### Foyot (Valois)
Béarnaise with a spoonful of meat glaze (glace de viande) stirred in for a deeper, meatier edge. Grilled meats and offal.

### Maltaise
Hollandaise flavoured with the juice and zest of blood oranges in place of some of the lemon. Almost always served with asparagus.

### Mousseline (Chantilly)
Hollandaise lightened by folding in softly whipped cream just before serving, roughly 1/2 cup per cup of sauce. Delicate with poached fish, asparagus and soufflés.

### Dijon (Moutarde)
Hollandaise with Dijon mustard whisked in off the heat. Good with salmon, chicken and eggs.

### Noisette
Hollandaise made with browned butter instead of plain melted butter, for a toasted, hazelnut flavour. Excellent with fish and with poached eggs.

## Tomate

The only mother sauce whose flavour comes mostly from a vegetable rather than from stock or dairy. Escoffier's version is a long way from a quick marinara: salt pork, mirepoix, a roux, white stock and a couple of hours of simmering, then passed through a food mill. That produces a smooth, savoury, slightly sweet sauce that sits under gratins and braised meats as comfortably as it does on pasta. The roux is optional in a modern kitchen, since good canned tomatoes reduce to plenty of body on their own, but the mirepoix and the long simmer are what separate this from plain tomato sauce.

**🛒 Ingredients**
- 2 oz (60 g) salt pork or bacon, finely diced, or 2 tbsp olive oil for a meatless version
- 1 medium onion, 1 carrot and 1 celery rib, all finely diced
- 2 cloves garlic, minced
- 2 tbsp all-purpose flour (optional, for the classic version)
- 1 can (28 oz / 800 g) whole peeled plum tomatoes, crushed by hand, or 3 lb (1.4 kg) ripe tomatoes, peeled and chopped
- 2 cups (500 ml) white stock (chicken or veal)
- Sachet: 1 bay leaf, 2 sprigs thyme, 6 parsley stems
- 1 tsp sugar, if the tomatoes are sharp
- Salt and pepper
- 2 tbsp butter, to finish

**🥣 Steps**
1. Render the salt pork in a heavy pot over medium heat until the fat runs and the pieces are lightly golden.
2. Add the onion, carrot, celery and garlic and sweat 8 to 10 minutes, until soft and sweet but not browned.
3. If using the flour, stir it in and cook 2 minutes to make a light roux with the rendered fat.
4. Add the tomatoes, stock, sachet and sugar. Bring to a simmer.
5. Simmer partly covered for 1 1/2 to 2 hours, stirring occasionally and scraping the bottom, until it has reduced by about a third and no longer tastes raw.
6. Remove the sachet, then pass the sauce through a food mill, or blend and sieve it. Season with salt and pepper.
7. Off the heat, swirl in the butter to round out the acidity and give it shine.

### Portugaise
Tomate with onion and garlic cooked in olive oil, plus a good quantity of chopped fresh tomato left slightly chunky, and parsley. Served with fish, eggs and stuffed vegetables.

### Provençale
Tomate cooked with olive oil, plenty of garlic and Provençal herbs (thyme, oregano, basil), sometimes with a strip of orange zest and a few olives. Grilled fish, lamb, ratatouille.

### Créole
Tomate with sweated onion, celery and green pepper, garlic, bay and thyme, and a hit of cayenne or hot sauce. The base of shrimp Créole.

### Milanaise
Tomate garnished with sautéed mushrooms and julienned ham, sometimes with a spoonful of demi-glace stirred in. Traditional with pasta and veal cutlets.

### Napolitaine
Tomate simmered with a little stock and finished with butter and grated Parmesan for a rounder, richer pour over pasta.

### Espagnole (tomato)
Not to be confused with the brown mother sauce of the same name. Tomate with onion, garlic, mushrooms and diced sweet peppers, all sautéed before they go in. Served with grilled meats and eggs.

### 🔑 Key tips
- **Warm liquid into warm roux.** Both at similar temperatures, added in stages and whisked smooth, is what prevents lumps. If you do get lumps, a stick blender or a sieve fixes them.
- **Simmer long enough to cook out the flour.** A roux-thickened sauce needs at least 10 minutes at a bare simmer, and it will taste pasty if you stop as soon as it thickens.
- **Season at the end** on anything that reduces. Espagnole, demi-glace and tomate all concentrate as they cook, so salt added early ends up doubled in the finished sauce.
- **Finish with cold butter off the heat** (*monter au beurre*). A tablespoon or two whisked in gives sheen and a rounder mouthfeel, and it is the single easiest upgrade to any of these.
- **Strain everything.** A pass through a fine sieve is what separates a home sauce from a restaurant one.
- **Skim.** Fat and starchy foam rise to the surface of a long simmer, and taking them off keeps the sauce clear and clean tasting.
- **Watch the heat on the emulsions.** Hollandaise and anything with an egg yolk liaison must never boil.
- **Freeze the labour-intensive ones.** Demi-glace and tomate freeze beautifully in ice-cube trays. Béchamel and velouté are quick enough to make fresh, and hollandaise cannot be saved at all.
- **Nappe is the target.** A finished sauce should coat the back of a spoon well enough that a finger drawn through it leaves a clean line that holds for a second.

> [!tip] Rule of thumb
> Every mother sauce is **one liquid plus one thickener**: milk, white stock or brown stock plus a roux of matching colour; butter plus egg yolk; tomatoes plus time. Get the base right and the child sauces are just an addition or two on top.
