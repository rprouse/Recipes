# Dehydrating Ground Beef

Backpackers call dehydrated ground beef "gravel", and they are not being unfair: dried on its own it never comes back. The fix is half a cup of breadcrumbs worked into the raw meat before it is cooked. The crumbs open up the structure so water can get back into the dried meat on the trail, and it rehydrates tender every time instead of staying hard. Ground oats or ground dehydrated chickpeas do the same job if you avoid gluten.

The other half of the method is fat. Only lean or extra lean beef in the 7% to 10% range is worth drying, because fat is what goes rancid in storage no matter how dry the meat around it is. Some fat cooks out, more of it beads up on the surface during drying, and you blot it away as you go.

This is a component, not a meal. Dry it separately and it will drop into anything: chili, pasta, potatoes, rice. Its poultry sibling is [[Dehydrating Ground Chicken]], which uses the same starch trick with an extra oven step for safety.

### 🛒 Ingredients
- Lean or extra lean ground beef, 7% to 10% fat - 1 lb / 454 g
- Fine breadcrumbs, ground oats, or ground dehydrated chickpeas - ½ C

### 🥣 Steps
1. For each pound of beef, sprinkle ½ cup of finely ground breadcrumbs over the meat, or substitute ½ cup ground oats or ground dehydrated chickpeas.
2. Work the breadcrumbs into the raw meat with your fingers.
3. Form the meat into a ball and set it aside for a few minutes.
4. Break the meat into small pieces and cook in a frying pan over medium-high heat until lightly browned and fully cooked, stirring continuously.
5. Remove from the heat and squeeze between paper towels to remove moisture.
6. Spread the small pieces of ground beef on nonstick dehydrator trays or silicone mesh sheets.
7. Dehydrate at 145°F (63°C) for approximately six hours. Ground beef will be hard when dry.
8. Once or twice during drying, move the meat around and squeeze it with a paper towel to remove oil. Wipe any oil off the nonstick sheets. Break any pieces that are drying slower than the rest in half.
9. Store in an air-tight container away from heat and light.

> [!warning] Never dry raw ground beef
> Plenty of dehydrator manuals tell you to put raw meat straight on the trays. It is not safe. Evaporative cooling means the meat itself may never reach the temperature on the dial, so Salmonella and E. coli can survive the whole dry. The USDA position is that beef reaches 160°F (72°C) before it goes in the dehydrator, which is exactly what step 4 is for.

> [!warning] Read the fat percentage, not the label
> Lean and extra lean run 7% to 10%, and grass fed beef usually lands there too. Anything sold as Ground Round is around 15%, and Chuck or plain Hamburger is higher still. Blot the meat with paper towels after cooking and a couple of times while it dries, because the fat you leave behind is what limits how long it keeps.

> [!note] Storage life
> Kept low fat and packed with care, dried beef holds up to a year in jars with oxygen absorbers or vacuum-sealed jars. Vacuum sealing and freezing pushes it to several years, since freezing stops the clock on everything biological. Let a bag come back to room temperature before opening it or condensation will form on the food.

> [!note] Numbers are estimates
> The source publishes no yield or nutrition figures, so `weight-per-serving`, `calories-per-serving` and `water-needed` are worked out from the ingredients: roughly 175 g dried out of a 1 lb batch, portioned at the ⅓ cup Chef Glenn uses in his own trail meals. Water is whatever the meal it goes into calls for; 120 ml is about what one portion of the meat alone takes back up.
