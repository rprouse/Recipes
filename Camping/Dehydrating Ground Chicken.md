# Dehydrating Ground Chicken

Chicken is the awkward one in the dehydrator. Chef Glenn tried baking, poaching, frying and grilling whole pieces and got the same result every time: tough dried chicken that never came back to tenderness on the trail. Ground poultry is the fix, and the trick is a starch. Work half a cup of fine breadcrumbs, ground oats, or ground dehydrated chickpeas into every pound of ground chicken or turkey before you cook it, and the dried crumbles rehydrate into meat that stays pleasantly chewy instead of turning to gravel. The starch holds onto water inside the meat, so it has somewhere to go back to.

The other half of the method is safety. Poultry has a looser cellular structure than beef, which leaves more places for pathogens to survive a low-temperature dry, so the meat gets cooked twice: once in a pan, then again in the oven. Because it goes into the dehydrator fully cooked, you dry it at 145°F rather than 160°F, which avoids case-hardening.

This is a component, not a meal. Bag it up and drop a third of a cup into any one-pot dinner in place of ground beef. The beef version is [[Dehydrating Ground Beef]], which uses the same starch trick without the second cook.

### 🛒 Ingredients
- Low fat ground chicken or turkey (98% fat-free ground breast) - 1 lb / 454 g
- Fine breadcrumbs, ground oats, or ground dehydrated chickpeas - ½ C
- Salt - ¼ tsp
- Pepper - ¼ tsp
- Ground cumin - ¼ tsp
- Cooking oil - 1 tsp

### 🥣 Steps
1. Season the starch: stir ¼ teaspoon each of salt, pepper, and ground cumin into the ½ cup of breadcrumbs.
2. Work the breadcrumbs into the meat a little at a time with your fingers.
3. Form the meat into a ball, set it aside for a few minutes, then pull it apart into small pieces. This is a sticky affair.
4. Preheat the oven to 350°F (180°C).
5. Lightly oil a non-stick pan with the teaspoon of cooking oil. Add all the meat at the same time and fry on medium-high for five to six minutes, until the meat is no longer pink. Stir and flip continuously with a spatula, chopping any larger pieces in half. Some of the meat will brown lightly, which is desirable.
6. Transfer the fried meat to a glass or ceramic baking dish and bake at 350°F (180°C) for ten minutes.
7. Turn the oven off but leave the meat inside for another ten minutes. Do not let any heat out by opening the door.
8. After the full twenty minutes in the oven, remove the meat and let it cool.
9. Once it is cool enough to handle, spread the meat out on dehydrator trays, tearing any larger pieces smaller.
10. Dehydrate for approximately six hours at 145°F (63°C).
11. Store in an oxygen and moisture-proof container away from heat and light.

> [!tip] Dry at 145°F, not 160°F
> Raw ground meat needs 160°F (70°C) to be safe, but this meat has already been cooked through twice. Drying it that hot risks case-hardening, where the outside seals and hardens while the middle stays moist. A sealed-in wet centre is exactly what spoils in storage.

> [!tip] Mesh sheets on top
> Chef Glenn puts the dehydrator's mesh sheets on top of the nonstick sheets rather than under them. Air circulates around the meat better that way, and the nonstick sheet underneath still catches the small bits that fall through.

> [!note] Storage life
> Properly cooked and dried ground poultry keeps up to a year in mason jars with oxygen absorbers, or vacuum-sealed with a jar-sealer attachment. Mylar or vacuum bags work too, and a sealed bag in the freezer stretches it further. Let frozen bags come back to room temperature before opening them, or condensation forms on the food.

> [!note] Gluten-free swaps
> Ground oats or ground dehydrated chickpeas substitute for breadcrumbs one for one, at ½ cup per pound, with no loss in how well the meat rehydrates. Grinding up chicken-flavoured hummus and using that as the starch makes the meat taste extra chickeny.

> [!warning] Fat is the enemy of shelf life
> Use only low fat ground poultry. Chef Glenn buys 98% fat-free ground breast. Fat goes rancid in storage no matter how dry the meat is around it, which is also why thigh meat is a poor choice here.

> [!note] Numbers are estimates
> The source gives no yield or nutrition figures for the ground poultry, so `weight-per-serving`, `calories-per-serving` and `water-needed` are worked out from the ingredients: roughly 170 g dried out of a 1 lb batch, portioned at the ⅓ cup Chef Glenn uses in his own trail meals. Water is whatever the meal it goes into calls for; 120 ml is about what one portion of the meat alone takes back up.
