60 g carbs and gets you close on sodium for one hour in moderate heat. Scale to 90 g (60 malto / 30 fructose) if you're pushing higher intakes and your gut tolerates it.

Glucose and fructose use different intestinal transporters, so combining them lets you absorb more per hour than glucose alone - the 2:1 ratio is what the research settled on. Maltodextrin is just a glucose polymer, and it's less sweet and less osmotically aggressive than pure glucose, which is why it's the standard base.

Plain table sugar is sucrose, already glucose and fructose in a 1:1 ratio, and it performs nearly as well in the studies. 60 g sugar plus 1/4 tsp salt in a bottle is a perfectly good mix and costs almost nothing. Maltodextrin's real advantage is taste - 60 g of sugar in 500 mL is cloying.

> Maltodextrin is a standard brewing ingredient (used for body in beer), sold in 1-5 kg bags, and it's usually the cheapest source per kg because it isn't marketed as a supplement. Same food-grade corn-derived powder.

### 🛒 Ingredients
- 40 g maltodextrin
- 20 g fructose
- Or replace the maltodextrin/fructose with 60g table sugar
- 1/4 tsp table salt (~575 mg sodium)
- Squeeze of lemon or lime, or a pinch of unsweetened drink flavour
### 🥣 Steps
1. Mix in a 500ml bottle
### 🗒️ Notes
- Sodium citrate instead of table salt if the salty taste bothers you; it's gentler on the stomach and about 1/3 sodium by weight, so use roughly 1.7 g for the same effect
- Potassium and magnesium are mostly marketing at these timescales, but lite salt covers both if you want them
- Pre-mix dry batches in ziplocs or a jar and add water at the ride - much easier than measuring at 6 am
- Mix fresh on hot days; sugary bottles grow things fast, and rinse immediately after
- Higher concentrations (say 90-120 g in one bottle) work for a lot of people if you drink plain water alongside, but introduce it in training, never on the first day of a big block
### 🍼 Comparison to other sports drinks

Nuun Sport has,
- 300mg Sodium
- 4g Carbs
- 13mg Calcium
- 150mg Potassium
- 25mg Magnesium
- 40mg Chloride

|Mineral|Sweat loss per litre|Nuun per 500 mL|Verdict|
|---|---|---|---|
|Sodium|500-1500 mg|300 mg|The one that matters, and undersupplied|
|Chloride|800-1400 mg|40 mg|Matters, but comes free with table salt|
|Potassium|150-300 mg|150 mg|Adequately covered, and barely needed anyway|
|Magnesium|under 5 mg|25 mg|Irrelevant intra-ride|
|Calcium|20-40 mg|13 mg|Irrelevant intra-ride|

**Potassium**

Losses are genuinely small, and more importantly your plasma potassium tends to _rise_ during hard exercise, not fall - glycogen breakdown and muscle contraction push potassium out of cells faster than sweat removes it. Total body potassium is enormous compared to what a ride costs you. A banana or a handful of dates covers a day's worth. So it's not harmful to include, it's just not solving a problem. If you want it in the mix, 1/8 tsp of lite salt (potassium chloride blend) adds a couple hundred milligrams and costs nothing.

**Magnesium**

Sweat magnesium is a trace, well under 5 mg per litre, so 25 mg is dosed for the label rather than for you. The cramping-magnesium link that sells a lot of tablets doesn't hold up - current thinking is that exercise-associated cramps are neuromuscular fatigue, not electrolyte depletion. Magnesium status does matter chronically and a lot of people run low dietarily, but that's a dinner-plate question, not a bottle question.

**The chloride detail worth noticing**

Nuun listing 300 mg sodium but only 40 mg chloride tells you most of their sodium comes from bicarbonate or carbonate, not salt. Table salt is 40% sodium and 60% chloride by weight, so your quarter teaspoon delivers ~575 mg sodium plus ~885 mg chloride automatically. That's closer to actual sweat composition than the tablet is.

**Protein during the ride**

Skip it. When carbohydrate intake is adequate, adding protein to the bottle doesn't reliably improve endurance performance, and it has two real costs: it slows gastric emptying and raises GI distress risk, and it displaces carbs from a bottle where carb density is the constraint. There's some evidence for reduced muscle damage markers, but the performance data is equivocal and the mechanism overlaps with just eating protein afterward.

The exception is very long efforts - past six hours or so, some riders find a little protein helps with satiety and with the palate fatigue that pure sugar causes. If you're doing all-day epics, worth testing. For four to six hour days, keep protein in the post-ride shake where it belongs.

So the practical answer: your DIY mix isn't missing anything meaningful. Add lite salt if you want the potassium for peace of mind, and don't bother chasing magnesium or protein in the bottle.
